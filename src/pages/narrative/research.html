SFU — Research Software Contributor

Computational geometry applied to biomechanics — where engineering practice meets research problems.

Simon Fraser University (Jul 2026 — Present)

Research was the first time I worked on a problem where no reference implementation existed to compare against.

I develop a Python pipeline that registers skin meshes to underlying muscle and bone structures, working with anatomical meshes of 16,000+ vertices across 43 structures.

The work sits at the intersection of computational geometry and software engineering: signed distance functions, mesh deformation, collision handling, K-nearest surface blending, and Laplacian smoothing, all wired into Autodesk Maya so that modelling workflows can run automatically instead of by hand.

Production code has requirements you can look up. Research code has requirements you have to discover.

Correctness here is geometric and visual, so progress depends on building tooling that makes results inspectable before it depends on writing more algorithms.

That shifted how I approach engineering in general: define the evaluation loop first, keep every step reproducible, and treat the pipeline itself as the product.

Problem space

  • Anatomical registration
    Attaching skin meshes to muscle and bone structures accurately enough for biomechanical simulation.
  • Scale
    16,000+ vertex meshes across 43 anatomical structures, where manual attachment does not scale.

How I contribute

  • Geometry algorithms
    Signed distance functions, mesh deformation, and collision handling for anatomically valid attachment.
  • Automation
    Python tooling around Autodesk Maya that turns a manual modelling workflow into a repeatable pipeline.
  • Evaluation
    Comparing registration and smoothing strategies to see which produce stable, physically plausible results.

This stage connects the engineering habits I built in industry with problems that are still open — and it is where I learned how much of research is really careful software.
